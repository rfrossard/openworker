# Presentation art direction

Use this taste pass after the storyboard and again after rendering. Judge the deck as a
sequence, not as isolated slides.

## Choose the visual system

1. Match the template to the communication job:
   - Essential for executive, academic, and operational clarity.
   - Editorial & cultural for narrative, brand, workshop, and heritage material.
   - Photographic for human, environmental, scientific, and documentary stories.
   - Data & evidence for metrics, comparisons, research, and financial decisions.
   - Expressive only when energy or memorability is more important than formality.
2. Use one template for the deck. Do not mix visual systems to create variety.
3. Use one dominant neutral, one accent, and at most one supporting color. Treat
   gradients as atmosphere, not decoration behind every object.
4. Prefer a POTX when the user supplies a real brand or institutional system.

## Establish hierarchy

- Deck title: 50–64 pt, normally no more than 10 words.
- Slide title: 35–44 pt, preferably one line and never more than two.
- Subheading or key message: 24–30 pt.
- Body: 16–22 pt; captions and sources: 10–13 pt.
- Use no more than two font families and three weights.
- Shorten copy before reducing font size. Avoid orphaned words and near-empty second lines.
- Keep alignment intentional. Do not center body copy unless the slide is a statement,
  quotation, or deliberate chapter break.

## Build rhythm

- Open with a minimal cover, then establish context before adding detail.
- Alternate silhouettes: text-led, visual-led, data-led, and transition slides.
- Never repeat the same layout more than twice consecutively.
- Limit card grids to at most 20% of the deck. Prefer one composition over many small boxes.
- Use section slides only at genuine narrative turns.
- Place the most visually expressive slide near an important argument, not at random.
- Close by resolving the opening question; do not end on a generic summary or “Thank you.”

For a typical 10-slide deck, prefer this cadence:

1. Minimal cover
2. Context or tension
3. Evidence or human example
4. Key metric or comparison
5. Section turn
6. Explanation or process
7. Visual proof
8. Options or implications
9. Recommendation
10. Decision or next action

Adapt the cadence to the story; do not force an agenda slide when the narrative is already clear.

## Compose with taste

- Give each slide one dominant element and one clear reading path.
- Preserve 5–8% outer margins and align elements to a small number of invisible guides.
- Use whitespace to separate meaning, not to leave accidental holes.
- Keep text blocks narrower than the full canvas. Aim for 45–75 characters per line.
- Use full-bleed imagery only when it can carry the slide; otherwise give it a deliberate frame.
- Crop around the subject and preserve negative space where text will sit.
- Use tables for exact lookup, charts for patterns, and large numbers for one decisive metric.
- Avoid decorative charts, unlabelled axes, tiny legends, and invented data.
- Use animation only to clarify sequence or reveal; prefer one transition family per deck.

## Rendered-deck taste audit

Inspect every slide at full size and the contact sheet. Record pass, revise, or reject for:

1. Narrative order — each slide creates the need for the next.
2. Hierarchy — the title, claim, evidence, and source are distinguishable instantly.
3. Legibility — typography meets the scale above without clipping or awkward wrapping.
4. Composition — the slide has one focal point and balanced visual weight.
5. Consistency — palette, typography, margins, image treatment, and numbering belong together.
6. Diversity — adjacent slides have purposeful silhouette changes without visual chaos.
7. Restraint — no unnecessary cards, lines, icons, gradients, or animation.
8. Evidence — charts, images, quotations, and claims are accurate and traceable.

Reject the deck if any slide has unreadable text, unresolved placeholders, accidental overlap,
weak contrast, a misleading visual, or a repetitive title-and-bullets rhythm.
